# Affect → face and posture mapping for the robot face: literature and timescales

This builds on EMOTION_LITERATURE.md §7–8 and does not repeat it. I checked every DOI below against Crossref metadata (authors, year, title, journal, volume and pages all matched). **[abs]** means I also read the abstract (Europe PMC, Semantic Scholar or OpenAlex). **[sec]** means the specific claim comes only from secondary sources or search summaries. **[mem]** means it comes from my own knowledge and was not checked this session.

## 1. Valence: brow (corrugator, AU4) versus smile (zygomaticus, AU12) and cheek (orbicularis oculi, AU6)

- **Cacioppo, Petty, Losch & Kim (1986)**, *JPSP* 50:260–268, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.50.2.260 **[abs]**. EMG over the brow (corrugator), eye (orbicularis oculi) and cheek (zygomaticus) separated both the *valence* and the *intensity* of mild affect. Judges watching video of the same faces could not. Facial EMG is a "continuous probe" of affect that is too subtle to see. For a robot, this means valence should show **graded**, not all-or-nothing.
- **Lang, Greenwald, Bradley & Hamm (1993)**, *Psychophysiology* 30:261–273, doi:10.1111/j.1469-8986.1993.tb03352.x **[abs]**. Over 6 s of picture viewing, facial EMG covaried with **valence** ratings and skin conductance with **arousal** ratings. Interest and viewing time also followed arousal. This is the classic double dissociation: face = valence, autonomic = arousal. That zygomaticus also rises somewhat for very unpleasant pictures is **[mem]**.
- **Larsen, Norris & Cacioppo (2003)**, *Psychophysiology* 40:776–785, doi:10.1111/1469-8986.00078 **[abs]**. With pictures, sounds and words (N = 68), valence had a **stronger linear effect on corrugator than on zygomaticus**. Positive and negative affect had **reciprocal** effects on corrugator (it relaxes below baseline for pleasant stimuli and contracts for unpleasant ones) but not on zygomaticus.
- **Answer to "is corrugator bipolar and linear?"** Yes, approximately. Corrugator is the best single *bipolar*, near-linear valence channel. Zygomaticus is effectively *unipolar*: it mostly signals positive affect and saturates or misbehaves at the extremes (grimaces).
- **Design consequence:** drive **brow lowering / inner-brow angle from V linearly over its whole range**, and drive **smile/mouth curvature mainly from V⁺**, with a small, separate frown term for V⁻. Orbicularis oculi (AU6, cheek raise that narrows the lower lid) marks *enjoyment* smiles. Frank, Ekman & Friesen (1993), *JPSP* 64:83–93, doi:10.1037/0022-3514.64.1.83 **[abs]**: observers detected enjoyment smiles better when the orbicularis oculi action was more salient. On an eyes-only face, positive V can therefore be shown as an **upward-curved lower eyelid ("smiling eyes")**.

## 2. Arousal: eye aperture, pupil, blink and movement speed

- **Pupil.** Bradley, Miccoli, Escrig & Lang (2008), *Psychophysiology* 45:602–607, doi:10.1111/j.1469-8986.2008.00654.x **[abs]**. After the initial light reflex, dilation was larger for arousing pictures, **whether pleasant or unpleasant**, and covaried with skin conductance (sympathetic). The pupil is an **arousal-only, valence-blind** channel. Partala & Surakka (2003), *IJHCS* 59:185–198, doi:10.1016/S1071-5819(03)00017-X, is consistent with this (metadata only). The pupil–LC link (Joshi et al. 2016, *Neuron* 89:221–234, doi:10.1016/j.neuron.2015.11.028) is already covered in §7.
- **Eye widening (AU5) and its sensory function.** Susskind et al. (2008), *Nat. Neurosci.* 11:843–850, doi:10.1038/nn.2138 **[abs]**. Fear and disgust have *opposite* shape features. Posed fear enlarged the subjective visual field, sped up eye movements, and increased nasal volume and inspiratory air speed. Disgust did the reverse. Widened eyes therefore mean **"increase sensory intake"** (high arousal, vigilance) and narrowed eyes mean **"reject or reduce intake"**. This is a principled basis for **lid aperture ∝ arousal**, with narrowing reserved for aversion and pain.
- **Blink rate.** Maffei & Angrilli (2019), *Physiol. Behav.* 204:256–263, doi:10.1016/j.physbeh.2019.02.037 **[abs]**. Blinks were *inhibited* most by engaging or attention-holding clips (erotic, scenery, compassion) and least by fear clips. The main predictor was valence and attention, not arousal per se. Use **blink suppression for engaged attention** (for example, during information gain). Do not model blink rate as a monotonic function of arousal.
- **Movement speed.** Pollick, Paterson, Bruderlin & Sanford (2001), *Cognition* 82:B51–B61, doi:10.1016/S0010-0277(01)00147-0 **[abs]**. Affect perceived from point-light arm movements formed a circumplex. The activation (arousal) dimension was **directly correlated with kinematics (speed)**, while pleasantness depended on the phase relations between limb segments. For a pendulum robot, **arousal → speed and jerk of eye saccades and body sway** is well grounded. Valence is not readable from speed alone.
- **Voice.** Covered in §7 (Juslin & Laukka 2003).

## 3. Dominance, control and confidence

- **Head tilt.** Mignault & Chaudhuri (2003), *J. Nonverbal Behav.* 27:111–132, doi:10.1023/A:1023914509763 (metadata only). That a raised head reads as dominant/superior and a bowed head as submissive (sad, ashamed) is **[sec + mem]**. Witkower & Tracy (2019), *Psychol. Sci.* 30:893–906, doi:10.1177/0956797619838762 **[abs]** (5 studies, N = 1,517): **with gaze held forward**, a *downward* head tilt *increased* perceived dominance. The cause is an illusory lowered, V-shaped brow, an "action-unit imposter" for AU4. **Implication:** "chin down" plus direct gaze reads as threat or dominance, and "chin down" plus averted or downcast gaze reads as submission. On a screen face, **dominance should be carried by gaze direction plus the brow/lid shape**, not by vertical offset alone.
- **Gaze.** Adams & Kleck (2005), *Emotion* 5:3–11, doi:10.1037/1528-3542.5.1.3 **[abs]**. Direct gaze enhances approach emotions (anger, joy) and averted gaze enhances avoidance emotions (fear, sadness) ("shared signal"). **Map D⁺ → direct, steady gaze and D⁻ → averted or downcast gaze with more gaze shifts.**
- **Pride and shame.** Tracy & Robins (2004), *Psychol. Sci.* 15:194–197, doi:10.1111/j.0956-7976.2004.01503008.x **[abs]**: pride = small smile, **head tilted slightly back**, expanded posture, arms raised or hands on hips. Tracy & Matsumoto (2008), *PNAS* 105:11655–11660, doi:10.1073/pnas.0802686105 **[abs]**: sighted, blind and **congenitally blind** athletes from more than 30 nations showed pride displays after victory and shame displays after defeat, so both are likely innate. Culture damped the shame display in sighted athletes from individualistic cultures. Shame = head down, slumped or narrowed posture **[sec: Witkower & Tracy 2018 review, *Emot. Rev.* 11:184–193, doi:10.1177/1754073917749880, abstract lists pride/shame as bodily emotions]**. **For us:** after success (high empowerment plus positive δ), lean "up and expanded". After failure or a fall, lean "down and contracted".
- **Emotion → dominance inference.** Knutson (1996), *J. Nonverbal Behav.* 20:165–182, doi:10.1007/BF02281954 **[sec]**: anger → high dominance; fear and sadness → low dominance; happiness → high affiliation. Hess, Adams & Kleck (2005), *Cogn. Emot.* 19:515–536, doi:10.1080/02699930441000364 **[abs]**: anger displays are expected from people perceived as dominant. So **anger and fear differ mainly on D** (both are V⁻ and A⁺). That makes D essential for a VA-only face to tell "frustrated/blocked" from "scared".
- **PAD.** Russell & Mehrabian (1977), *J. Res. Pers.* 11:273–294, doi:10.1016/0092-6566(77)90037-X; Mehrabian (1996), *Curr. Psychol.* 14:261–292, doi:10.1007/BF02686918 (metadata only). This is the 3-factor basis for our (V, A, D).
- **Caveat.** Hall, Coats & LeBeau (2005), *Psychol. Bull.* 131:898–924, doi:10.1037/0033-2909.131.6.898 **[abs]**: *beliefs* about dominance cues (posture, gaze and so on) are much stronger and more common than *actual* links between dominance and behaviour. That is fine for a robot, which only needs to be read correctly. It is a reason not to claim the mapping is "how dominance really looks".
- **Animal defeat and submission.** Schenkel (1967), *Am. Zool.* 7:319–329, doi:10.1093/icb/7.2.319 **[abs]**: wolves and dogs show **active submission** (derived from food-begging) and **passive submission** (the posture of a cub being cleaned: lying on the back). Resident–intruder testing scores the intruder's "defensive upright posture, submission and freezing" (Koolhaas et al. 2013, JoVE, https://www.jove.com/v/4367 **[sec]**). The repeated social defeat protocol is Golden et al. (2011), *Nat. Protoc.* 6:1183–1191, doi:10.1038/nprot.2011.361 (metadata only). How these postures look in detail (lowered body, flattened ears, tail tucked, freezing) is **[mem]**. Learned-helplessness posture specifically: no primary source verified.

## 4. Pain

- **Human pain face.** Prkachin (1992), *Pain* 51:297–306, doi:10.1016/0304-3959(92)90213-U **[abs]**: across shock, cold, pressure and ischaemia, four actions carried "the bulk" of the information: **brow lowering (AU4), lid tightening/closing (AU6/7, AU43), and nose wrinkle / upper-lip raise (AU9/10)**. Prkachin & Solomon (2008), *Pain* 139:267–274, doi:10.1016/j.pain.2008.04.010 **[abs]**: brow lowering, orbit tightening, levator contraction and **eye closing** formed a unitary action, the basis of the PSPI index, which has test-retest reliability. The PSPI formula, AU4 + max(AU6, AU7) + max(AU9, AU10) + AU43, is **[mem]**. Kunz, Meixner & Lautenbacher (2019, online 2018), *Pain* 160:535–549, doi:10.1097/j.pain.0000000000001424 **[abs]**: the consistent core set is AU4, AU6/7, AU9/10 and **mouth opening (AU25/26/27)**. Eye closure (AU43) is more frequent in *clinical* pain, and individuals combine subsets rather than showing one fixed face.
- **Function.** Williams (2002), *Behav. Brain Sci.* 25:439–455, doi:10.1017/S0140525X02000080 **[abs]**: the pain face is evolved, consistent across stimuli and ages, and only partly under voluntary control. It "demands attention and prioritises escape, recovery and healing".
- **Animal grimace scales.** Langford et al. (2010), *Nat. Methods* 7:447–449, doi:10.1038/nmeth.1455 **[abs]**. The Mouse Grimace Scale has five action units, each scored 0–2: **orbital tightening, nose bulge, cheek bulge, ear position (back and apart), whisker change** **[sec]**. It works for noxious stimuli of *moderate duration*, so grimace is a tonic pain signal, not a reflex. Rat Grimace Scale: Sotocinal et al. (2011), *Mol. Pain* 7:55, doi:10.1186/1744-8069-7-55 **[abs]**, tracked the time course and morphine dose-response. Feline Grimace Scale: Evangelista et al. (2019), *Sci. Rep.* 9:19128, doi:10.1038/s41598-019-55693-8 (metadata only).
- **Cross-species invariant for pain: squeezed or closed eyes plus flattened or rotated "ears".** Pain narrows the eyes, which is the opposite of the arousal-driven widening above. For the robot, **pain must override arousal on lid aperture**: high arousal plus pain gives *squinted* eyes, not wide ones. That is how pain stays distinguishable from fear (fear = wide).
- **Protective responses.** Withdrawal reflexes are covered in §8. On the display: a fast flinch or blink at pain onset, then sustained squint, brow-lowering and "ears back", with posture guarding (reduced, cautious motion).

## 5. Discrete emotions in VA(D) space and the generative principles

- **Russell (1980)**, *JPSP* 39:1161–1178, doi:10.1037/h0077714 (metadata only): the circumplex (pleasure × arousal).
- **Appraisal → action units.** Scherer & Ellgring (2007), *Emotion* 7:113–130, doi:10.1037/1528-3542.7.1.113 **[abs]**: 12 actors × 14 emotions showed "little evidence for emotion-specific prototypical affect programs". The data favour **appraisal-driven, componential** configurations. Scherer (2009), *Cogn. Emot.* 23:1307–1351, doi:10.1080/02699930902928969 **[abs]**: appraisal checks run as a *sequence* (novelty → intrinsic pleasantness → goal conduciveness → coping potential/power → norms), and the response components synchronise. The specific predicted action units (novelty → AU1+2+5; goal obstruction → AU4; high power → AU4/7/23 with direct gaze; low power → AU1/26, gaze aversion) are **[mem]**. Search found only partial empirical support (Mortillaro et al. 2011: suddenness ↔ AU1/2) **[sec]**.
- **This architecture fits our signals.** Novelty/surprise ≈ a phasic arousal/surprise signal → brows up and eyes wide. Pleasantness/goal conduciveness ≈ V (δ) → brow and mouth. Coping potential/power ≈ **D (empowerment)** → gaze and head.
- **Temporal hierarchy.** Jack, Garrod & Schyns (2014), *Curr. Biol.* 24:187–192, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2013.11.064 **[abs]**: dynamic expressions first send a few "biologically basic" signals that support coarse categories such as approach/avoid. Socially specific signals come *later*. The robot face can do the same: **eye aperture and brow onset first (A and coarse V), mouth and posture later.**
- **Principle of antithesis.** Darwin (1872), *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*, ch. 2, http://darwin-online.org.uk/ **[mem; URL not opened]**: opposite states get opposite expressions. Darwin's example is the dog: a hostile dog is erect and stiff, tail up, hair bristling, ears forward; an affectionate dog is lowered and sinuous, tail wagging, ears back. **Design rule:** make each axis's two poles *geometric opposites* (up/down, open/closed, toward/away), so a 1-D signal is readable from either end.
- **Practical VAD placements** (the standard PAD octant labelling; [mem]): joy V⁺A⁺D⁺; pride V⁺A~D⁺⁺; relaxed/content V⁺A⁻D⁺; anger V⁻A⁺D⁺; fear V⁻A⁺D⁻; sadness/defeat V⁻A⁻D⁻; boredom V⁻A⁻D~; surprise V~A⁺⁺ (phasic).

## 6. Animals: ears, tail, posture (for an abstract body and ears)

- **Framework.** Mendl, Burman & Paul (2010), *Proc. R. Soc. B* 277:2895–2904, doi:10.1098/rspb.2010.0303 **[abs]**: core affect in animals is valence × arousal. Long-term **mood arises from accumulated short-term emotions** and in turn biases them, in both directions. This is a direct justification for a two-layer (emotion/mood) display.
- **Sheep ears.** Reefmann et al. (2009), *Appl. Anim. Behav. Sci.* 118:199–207, doi:10.1016/j.applanim.2009.02.013 **[sec]**: **frequent ear-posture changes accompany negative situations**, while a **high proportion of passive (relaxed, hanging) ears** accompanies positive ones. Raised tails did not separate valence. Boissy et al. (2011, *Anim. Welf.* 20:47–56) **[sec, DOI not verified]**: ears point **backward** in unfamiliar, unpleasant and *uncontrollable* situations. That last point ties ear position to **controllability (D)**. **Takeaway:** for abstract "ears", use *jitter/change rate* ∝ negative arousal, *relaxed droop* = calm positive, *back/flat* = low control or pain, and *forward and up* = alert/approach.
- **Dog tail.** Quaranta, Siniscalchi & Vallortigara (2007), *Curr. Biol.* 17:R199–R201, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2007.02.008 **[sec]**: wagging is biased to the **right** for positive stimuli (owner) and to the **left** for a threatening unfamiliar dog. Siniscalchi et al. (2013), *Curr. Biol.* 23:2279–2282, doi:10.1016/j.cub.2013.09.027 (metadata only): dogs respond differently to left- versus right-biased wagging in other dogs. **For the pendulum:** a small **left/right asymmetry of the idle sway** could carry valence in a biologically motivated way. This is optional and subtle.
- **Cats.** Bennett, Gourkow & Mills (2017), *Behav. Processes* 141:342–350, doi:10.1016/j.beproc.2017.03.011 **[abs]**: in shelter cats (CatFACS), fear went with **blinking and half-blinking** and a left head/gaze bias. Frustration went with hissing, nose-licking, jaw drop, lip raise and nose wrinkle. Relaxed engagement went with a right gaze/head bias. **Half-blinks are a cheap "anxious/low-D" cue for an LED face.**
- **Chimpanzees.** ChimpFACS, Parr et al. (2007), *Emotion* 7:172–181, doi:10.1037/1528-3542.7.1.172 (metadata only), confirms muscle-level homology, so the human action-unit logic transfers.
- **Posture and the vertical axis** (Darwin, Schenkel, Tracy): up, tall and expanded = dominant/confident; down, low and contracted = submissive/defeated. For a self-balancing pendulum, D could map to **lean set-point or "height"** (a slight backward/upright lean versus a forward slump), if the controller allows a small bias. Otherwise map it to the screen's vertical eye position plus gaze.

**Phasic versus tonic integration.**
- *Phasic* signals (δ, surprise, pain onset) are brief transients of about 100–500 ms and should be shown as *impulses*: a fast attack, then exponential decay.
- *Tonic* signals are **leaky integrals of the phasic ones** (Rutledge, Eldar) that relax toward a home base with a set attractor strength (Kuppens). Mood also feeds back on how new events are shown (Mendl, Eldar).
- Implementation: m_{t+1} = m_t + (Δt/τ_m)(h − m_t) + k·δ_t, and display = phasic(δ) + expression(V, A) + offset(m). Use **asymmetric attack and decay** (fast rise, slow fall), as in Ekman-style onset < offset and in emotion duration data.

**Recommended time constants.** These are our design choices, informed by the above. The control step is ~83 ms (12 Hz), so render at ≥30 Hz and interpolate.

| Layer | Driven by | Attack | Decay τ |
|---|---|---|---|
| L0 reflex/flash | startle, pain onset, large \|δ\|, surprise | 1 frame (≤50 ms) | 150–300 ms (blink ~150 ms; flash ~250 ms) |
| L1 expression | V, A, pain intensity, brow/lid/mouth | τ_on 150–300 ms (expressions visible by ~300–400 ms) | τ_off 0.8–1.5 s (total episode 0.5–4 s) |
| L1b pupil | A (and pain) | through an Erlang/gamma filter, t_peak ≈ 0.9 s | ~1–2 s |
| L2 posture/stance | D (empowerment), V-mood | τ ≈ 3–10 s | τ ≈ 10–30 s (asymmetric: slumping slower to recover than to onset after failure) |
| L3 mood/home base | leaky integral of δ and pain | — | τ ≈ 1–5 min (one session ≈ hours ↔ mood; scale to episode length) |

## Summary: recommendations for a minimal LED face (eyes, optional mouth)

| Affect dimension | Face / eye features | Head / body ("ears", sway, lean) | Onset / decay |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Valence +** | lower lid curved up ("smiling eyes", AU6 analogue); brows relaxed or raised; mouth curved up (V⁺ only); warm hue | relaxed, slow, smooth sway; ears relaxed/droopy (sheep "passive" ears) | expression τ_on ~0.2 s, τ_off ~1 s; mood offset τ ~min |
| **Valence −** | brow inner ends lowered and angled, ∝ \|V\| (corrugator analogue: **linear and bipolar across the whole V range**); mouth flat or down (weak); cool hue | ears change frequently (Reefmann); slight hunch | same; corrugator-like brow is the primary, continuous V channel |
| **Arousal** | lid aperture ∝ A (AU5; Susskind); pupil ∝ A (valence-blind; Bradley 2008); saccade rate and speed ∝ A; blink suppressed when *engaged* | sway amplitude and speed ∝ A (Pollick 2001); ears forward and up when alert | pupil: gamma filter, peak ~0.9 s; aperture ~0.2–0.3 s; surprise flash = brows up + eyes wide, decay ~0.3 s |
| **Dominance + (high empowerment)** | direct, steady gaze; slightly lowered upper lids with a flat brow (a "confident" look; with direct gaze, a lowered brow reads as dominant: Witkower & Tracy 2019) | upright or back-leaning, "tall", expanded (pride: Tracy & Robins 2004) | posture τ ~3–10 s; pride burst after success ~2–4 s |
| **Dominance − (low control)** | averted or downcast gaze, more gaze shifts; half-blinks (cat fear); inner brow raised | forward slump, lowered, contracted; ears back/flat (Boissy 2011 [sec]); shame posture after failure (Tracy & Matsumoto 2008) | onset ~3–5 s, recovery slower, τ ~10–30 s |
| **Pain** | **squint/eye closure** that *overrides* arousal widening (AU6/7/43); brow lowered (AU4); mouth open or tense (AU25–27; AU9/10 as a "snarl" bend); a fast blink at onset | ears back and apart (Mouse Grimace Scale); guarded, reduced motion; flinch | reflex flinch/blink ≤50 ms; grimace τ_on ~0.3 s, persists with nociception, τ_off ~1–3 s; residual "sore" mood τ ~min |

**Key design rules from the literature:**
1. Brow carries V linearly, and the mouth carries mostly V⁺.
2. Lid aperture and pupil carry A. Pain and disgust narrow the eyes, which breaks the tie between fear and pain.
3. D is carried by gaze direction combined with head/body height. Head tilt alone is ambiguous without gaze.
4. Make the poles of each axis antithetic (Darwin).
5. Show coarse signals first (eyes, brows) and finer ones later (mouth, posture) (Jack et al. 2014).
6. Use three or four layers with asymmetric attack and decay, and drive mood as a leaky integral of δ and pain with an attractor (Rutledge; Kuppens).
